# Reflection, diffusion and focusing (反射、擴散與聚焦)

**In one sentence:** the shape of a room decides where sound reflections go, and good halls spread them evenly instead of concentrating them. [S12]

## Reflection basics
- Sound can be pictured as rays that reflect off surfaces: the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence. [S12]
- Concave (inward-curved) forms tend to focus sound, while convex (outward-curved) forms help spread it. [S12]
- Many acousticians avoid concave forms and add convex shapes to increase diffusion. [S12]
- The goal is to distribute sound energy evenly; areas where rays converge signal focusing and call for a shape change. [S12]

## Echoes
- An echo happens when there is an audible gap between the direct sound and its reflection. [S4]
- Flutter echoes are a risk in plain rectangular rooms with parallel walls. [S7]
- Statues and other structures in the Musikverein scatter sound, which helps avoid flutter echoes. [S7]
- Fan-shaped rooms are not prone to flutter echoes because their walls are not parallel. [S7]
- Vineyard halls' irregular layout helps avoid flutter echoes and focusing. [S7]

## Tools designers use
- A ceiling alone is unlikely to deliver sound evenly, so suspended reflectors are angled to send sound to different audience areas. [S7]
- Musicians need to hear themselves and each other, so reflectors are often placed beside and above the stage. [S7]
- Berlin Philharmonie examples: ten downward-curving "clouds" hang 12 m above the platform, a tent-like ceiling of three convex arches helps spread sound, and "pyramids" at the ceiling edge absorb low frequencies. [S8]
- In vineyard halls, the seating blocks' fronts can be shaped to reflect sound towards nearby seats and other blocks. [S10]
