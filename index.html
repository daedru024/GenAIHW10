Why does the shape of a room change how music sounds?

An introduction to concert hall types

A large gothic cathedral can take up to 9 seconds for sound to die away. A small conference room lets it fade almost at once. Good concert halls aim in between, at roughly 2 seconds.

Sound bounces

  • Think of sound as rays that bounce off surfaces: the angle it arrives at equals the angle it leaves at.
  • Curved-in (concave) surfaces tend to focus sound into hot spots. Curved-out (convex) surfaces spread it.
  • A designer wants even sound at every seat, so they look for places where rays converge and reshape the room.
  • An echo is a reflection that arrives late enough to be heard separately from the direct sound.
  • Suspended reflectors are angled to send sound to different parts of the audience.

Reverberation time: how long sound lingers

  • Reverberation time (RT) is how long sound takes to drop by 60 decibels after the source stops.
  • A very reverberant room sounds “live”; one with little reverberation sounds “dead”.
  • Longer RT makes music fuller but speech muddier.
  • Wallace Sabine began measuring it at Harvard in 1895.

Clear sound and surrounding sound

  • Clarity compares sound arriving in the first 80 milliseconds with the later sound. Late reflections blur the music, so clarity tends to fall as RT rises.
  • Spaciousness: sound arriving from the sides makes the source seem wider than it is. A lateral energy fraction above 0.25 is recommended.
  • Envelopment: listeners feel surrounded when many early side reflections arrive before the 80 ms mark.
  • That is why symphonic halls prioritise side reflections, and one reason rectangular halls are favoured.
  • In Vienna’s Musikverein, the narrow shape and thick plaster walls give strong side reflections.

Much of this research was done on classical music, not pop or rock.

Shoebox: Musikverein, Vienna

  • Shape: a plain rectangle with the stage at one end, often with balconies.
  • Sound: RT of about 2 s, high clarity, and a feeling of being immersed in sound from all directions.
  • Why: many early side reflections arrive between the direct sound and 80 ms.
  • Catch: parallel walls can cause flutter echoes unless something scatters the sound. Statues along the sides do this in Vienna.
  • Case study: opened 6 January 1870, designed by Theophil Hansen, with 1,744 seats plus standing room for about 300. Hansen relied on intuition because no acoustics studies existed.

Vineyard: Berlin Philharmonie

  • Shape: the audience sits on terraces rising around a central stage, like the slopes of a vineyard.
  • Idea: its architect reasoned that people naturally gather in circles to listen to music.
  • Case study: opened in 1963, designed by Hans Scharoun, with 2,440 seats.
  • Problem: on an open platform, musicians struggled to hear each other. Fixes: reflective surfaces around the platform and ten curved “clouds” hung 12 m above it.
  • Strengths: holds many people, RT near the symphonic ideal, and an irregular layout that avoids flutter echoes.
  • Costs: complicated and expensive, and sound varies a lot by seat.

A vineyard hall in Taiwan: Weiwuying

  • Location: Kaohsiung, in the National Kaohsiung Center for the Arts. Opened in 2018, with about 2,000 seats in the concert hall.
  • A vineyard-style hall by architect Francine Houben, with acoustics by Albert Yaying Xu.
  • Its design kept RT between 2.1 s (mid frequencies) and 2.52 s (low frequencies).
  • Side walls, ceiling height, materials, seating and stage were all designed with acoustics in mind.

No source in this Wiki describes the shape of Taipei’s National Concert Hall, so it is not classified here.

Fan-shaped: many seats, front-facing

  • Shape: the side walls open outward from the stage into a fan.
  • Why: it seats many people who all keep a frontal view of the performers.
  • Strengths: non-parallel walls avoid flutter echoes, and direct sound reaches the audience easily.
  • Weaknesses: RT is generally low, clarity can be very low, ensemble is hard for musicians, and a curved back wall can cause echoes.
  • Sources disagree on spaciousness: one says the wide rear gives it, another says there is little side energy so listeners do not feel surrounded.
  • Examples: Barbican Hall in London, and Tanglewood Music Shed in Massachusetts, which is fan-shaped yet acoustically successful thanks to reflective upper panels.

Horseshoe: La Scala, Milan

  • Shape: a U-shaped plan with several tiers of boxes, a pattern common in late 18th-century Italian theatres.
  • La Scala: the architect Giuseppe Piermarini built a plaster vault on wooden ribs with a gap above it, making a hollow space like the body of an instrument.
  • Sound: RT of about 1.5 s whatever the size, good balance between orchestra pit and stage, and high clarity.

Both sources on this type are about opera houses, so this is evidence for opera rather than symphony halls.
